\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Sparse attention methods decide which key--value pairs a query may see. Two families dominate: \emph{hash-based} methods, which bucket tokens by a random angular projection (Reformer), and \emph{cluster-based} methods, which bucket them by learned centroids (Routing Transformer). We revisit this design space from the geometry of the router itself, propose an architecture built on three choices, and then subject each of them to controlled numerical study against a reference implementation.

The architecture, PHASOR, routes tokens by a \emph{learned orthogonal} rotation acting on a \emph{fixed} orthonormal codebook; reads a routing subspace that rotary position embeddings do not touch; and makes the tokens a query's bucket excludes visible to the softmax as coarse residue summaries rather than skipping them.

The studies are the substance of this report, and two of them overturned claims we had made. We show that (i) the codebook geometry is provably rigid under training, which an unconstrained router's is not --- we drive a free router's minimum prototype angle from $48.4^\circ$ to $0^\circ$ while ours stays at exactly $90^\circ$; (ii) the bucket load is a function of the \emph{input distribution} and not of the router's parameters, so a balancing loss placed on the router cannot work, which invalidated one of our own planned ablations; (iii) rotary phase does not degrade angular routing toward uniform hashing as we had claimed, but toward a hard \emph{capacity ceiling} of five to ten effective buckets that is nearly independent of how many are provisioned --- a worse failure, with a geometric explanation: the routing input's trajectory over positions is confined to a torus of dimension $\dc/2$ and therefore explores a measure-zero subset of the sphere; and (iv) the bucket-sorted chunking that the method's $O(n^{1.5})$ complexity depends on is faithful only when the chunk size is comparable to the mean bucket size, retaining as little as $38\%$ of true in-bucket pairs otherwise, which couples the routing width to the context length and weakens the complexity claim.

We report no language-modelling results, and every table marked as such is a placeholder. What we do report is reproducible from the released code with a single command. We state precisely which components are novel, which are recombinations of published ideas, which of our own claims the evidence has already corrected, and which predictions would falsify what remains.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

The attention mechanism \citep{vaswani2017attention} computes, for every query, a distribution over every key. This is the operation that makes transformers general and the operation that makes them expensive. A long line of work reduces the cost by restricting \emph{which} keys each query sees: by fixed patterns \citep{child2019sparse,beltagy2020longformer,zaheer2020bigbird}, by random angular hashing \citep{kitaev2020reformer}, by online clustering \citep{roy2021routing}, or by learned block selection \citep{yuan2025nsa,lu2025moba}. A parallel line reduces cost by restricting \emph{which parameters} each token uses, through mixture-of-experts routing in the feed-forward layer \citep{shazeer2017outrageously,fedus2022switch,lepikhin2021gshard}, in the attention projections \citep{zhang2022moa,csordas2024switchhead,jin2025moh}, or across depth \citep{raposo2024mixture}.

These two lines share a component that receives comparatively little design attention: the \textbf{router}. In most systems the router is a single linear map followed by a softmax or a top-$k$, trained end to end with an auxiliary load-balancing loss. This is known to be brittle. \citet{chi2022representation} show that routing through a low-rank linear map drives expert prototypes toward one another --- \emph{representation collapse} --- and propose a cosine-similarity router with $L_2$-normalised prototypes as a remedy. \citet{zoph2022stmoe} document the stability problems that motivate router $z$-losses. \citet{roller2021hash} show that a \emph{fixed random hash} is a competitive router, which is an uncomfortable result for learned routing: it suggests that much of what learned routers contribute is partitioning, not semantics.

This report takes the geometry of the router as its primary object of study. Our starting observation is that the two most successful content-based sparse-attention mechanisms are both \emph{angular}. Reformer assigns buckets by $\arg\max$ over $[\bx \bR;\, -\bx \bR]$ for a \emph{random} rotation $\bR$ --- cross-polytope LSH \citep{andoni2015crosspolytope,charikar2002simhash}. Routing Transformer runs spherical $k$-means on $L_2$-normalised queries and keys, so its centroids are also directions. In both cases the quantity that determines routing is an angle. The difference is only whether the frame defining the sectors is random and fixed (Reformer) or data-fit and learned (Routing Transformer).

That framing suggests the intermediate design we study here. Let the frame be \emph{learned}, as in Routing Transformer, but constrained to be \emph{orthogonal}, as in Reformer. The router applies a learned rotation $\bR \in SO(\dc)$ to a normalised token representation and assigns the bucket by $\arg\max$ over a \emph{fixed} orthonormal codebook. We call the resulting attention layer \textbf{PHASOR} (\underline{Ph}ase-\underline{A}ware \underline{S}parse attention with \underline{O}rthogonal \underline{R}outing).

\subsection{The three design choices}

\paragraph{A constrained frame, not a free one.} If the codebook were also learned, the rotation would be redundant --- $\langle \bR\hat\bu, \bc_b\rangle = \langle \hat\bu, \bR^\top \bc_b\rangle$ --- and we would have reinvented a cosine router. Because the codebook is fixed and orthonormal and the learned map is constrained to $O(\dc)$, the \emph{geometry of the partition} is invariant under training: sectors stay equiangular and mutually separated by $\pi/2$ no matter what the optimiser does (Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse}, measured in Section~\ref{sec:s4}). Training moves the \emph{data} relative to a rigid partition rather than moving a deformable partition relative to the data.

\paragraph{Routing that does not read rotary dimensions.} Reformer and Routing Transformer were designed before rotary position embeddings \citep{su2024roformer} became the default. RoPE encodes position by \emph{rotating} query and key subvectors as a function of position, so an angular router reading RoPE-rotated features reads a phase that mixes content and position. We make this precise in Proposition~\ref{prop:rope} and measure it in Section~\ref{sec:s1}. The fix is structural and cheap: route on a subspace that RoPE does not touch. Partial application of RoPE is already standard practice for unrelated reasons \citep{black2022gptneox}, and decoupled RoPE dimensions are used in DeepSeek-V2's latent attention \citep{deepseekv2}; we give the routing-specific argument for it.

\paragraph{A fallback the softmax can see.} In Mixture-of-Depths \citep{raposo2024mixture} and in capacity-overflow handling in MoE layers \citep{fedus2022switch,lepikhin2021gshard}, an unselected token is carried forward by the residual stream and is otherwise \emph{absent} from the block. Information is preserved, but the excluded token is not merely down-weighted --- it is not present in the softmax at all, so attention cannot express ``I looked and it was irrelevant'' as distinct from ``I was not allowed to look''. We propose instead that the router's residue be made visible at low resolution: the tokens a query's bucket excludes are pooled into $B$ summary key--value pairs and appended to that query's key set, at $O(nB)$ cost. This is related to, but distinct from, global tokens \citep{beltagy2020longformer,zaheer2020bigbird}, attention sinks \citep{xiao2024streaming}, registers \citep{darcet2024registers}, and NSA's compression branch \citep{yuan2025nsa}: in all of those the coarse branch is \emph{independent} of the routing decision, whereas here the summaries are exactly the complement of what the router selected, and therefore carry a different signal for each query.

\subsection{What this report adds beyond the design}
\label{sec:contributions}

A design is cheap. We therefore built the reference implementation first and measured each claim before asserting it. That process changed the paper four times, and we report the changes rather than the polished end state, because the corrections are more informative than the original claims were.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Proposition~\ref{prop:load}, which we did not expect.} The bucket marginal under an orthogonal router with a fixed codebook is \emph{invariant to the router's parameters} whenever the routing input is isotropic. This is a stronger guarantee than Proposition~\ref{prop:collapse} and it is free. It also has a cost: it means a balancing loss whose gradient reaches only $\bR$ cannot change the load, which \textbf{invalidated ablation A6 as we had specified it} (Section~\ref{sec:s3}).

\item \textbf{A capacity ceiling, not uniform hashing.} We had claimed that routing on RoPE dimensions degenerates toward chance, $1/B$. Measurement shows the aggregate limit is roughly $0.14$--$0.20$ co-routing agreement regardless of $B$, because the router reaches only five to ten \emph{effective} buckets however many are provisioned --- $37\%$ of a 16-bucket codebook but only $8\%$ of a 128-bucket one. Proposition~\ref{prop:torus} explains why: the routing input's trajectory over positions lies on a torus of dimension $\dc/2$ inside a $\dc$-dimensional sphere, and therefore meets only a vanishing fraction of the partition's cells. The practical consequence is the opposite of reassuring: provisioning a larger codebook makes the relative collapse worse (Section~\ref{sec:s2}).

\item \textbf{The complexity claim is weaker than stated.} The $O(n^{1.5})$ figure assumes bucket-sorted fixed-size chunks. We measure how far that approximation is from true bucket-wise attention and find it is faithful only when the chunk size is comparable to the mean bucket size $n/B$. At $n = 4096$ with $B = 32$ and chunk $32$, the chunked pattern retains $38\%$ of true in-bucket pairs, with load entropy at $0.996$ --- so this is not a load-imbalance artefact. Since $B = 2\dc$ is tied to the head's content width, holding fidelity fixed as $n$ grows requires $\dc$ to grow with $\sqrt{n}$ (Section~\ref{sec:s7}).

\item \textbf{A null result that constrains the protocol.} At initialisation, all four ablation arms produce patterns of indistinguishable quality --- recall-over-density between $2.5$ and $4.7$ for every variant, on both unstructured and content-structured inputs. The cross-polytope partition beats a random pattern of equal density by a factor of three to five, but \emph{which} angular rule is used makes no measurable difference before training. Ablations A1 and A3 therefore cannot be settled by pattern geometry; they require training (Section~\ref{sec:s5}).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{What this report is and is not.} This is a method-and-evidence report, not a results paper. The components we build on are published, and Section~\ref{sec:positioning} maps each element of our design onto its closest antecedent, including cases where the antecedent is very close. We have trained no language model; every table of language-modelling results is a placeholder, marked as such. The evidence we do present is of one kind --- controlled measurement of the mechanism on constructed inputs against the reference implementation --- and Section~\ref{sec:studies-scope} states exactly what that kind of evidence can and cannot support. Section~\ref{sec:protocol} specifies the experiments that would settle the rest, revised in light of what the studies found, including the outcomes that would falsify each remaining claim.
